## Core Concepts
### Electromagnetism from First Principles
Electromagnetism arises from the unification of electric and magnetic phenomena described by Maxwell’s equations.  
- **Gauss’s law for electricity** relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge:  
  $$\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}= \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$  
  From this, the field of a point charge follows directly (see Worked Example 1).  
- **Gauss’s law for magnetism** states there are no magnetic monopoles:  
  $$\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}=0$$  
  Hence magnetic field lines form continuous loops.  
- **Faraday’s law** links a time‑varying magnetic flux to an induced electric field:  
  $$\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}= -\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$$  
- **Ampere‑Maxwell law** adds the displacement current to the magnetostatic Biot‑Savart law:  
  $$\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l}= \mu_0\!\left(I_{\text{enc}}+\varepsilon_0\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}\right)$$  

The **Lorentz force** on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ in fields $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ is  
$$\mathbf{F}=q\bigl(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\bigr)$$  
which follows from the relativistic transformation of the electromagnetic tensor $F^{\mu\nu}$.

### Electric Field
The electric field $\mathbf{E}$ is defined as the force per unit test charge:  
$$\mathbf{E}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{F}}{q}$$  
It is a **conservative** vector field ($\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=0$ for static charges) and can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar potential $V$:  
$$\mathbf{E}=-\nabla V$$  
The potential satisfies Poisson’s equation $\nabla^{2}V=-\rho/\varepsilon_0$, reducing to Laplace’s equation $\nabla^{2}V=0$ in charge‑free regions.

### Magnetic Field
The magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is defined via the force on a moving charge:  
$$\mathbf{B}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{F}}{q\,\mathbf{v}}\quad\text{(for }\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{B}\text{)}$$  
Its unit is the tesla ($\text{T}= \text{N}\,\text{s}\,\text{C}^{-1}\,\text{m}^{-1}$).  
Because $\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B}=0$, $\mathbf{B}$ is **solenoidal** and cannot be written as the gradient of a scalar potential; instead it is the curl of a vector potential $\mathbf{A}$:  
$$\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A},\qquad \nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{A}=0\ (\text{Coulomb gauge})$$

## How It Works
### Superposition and Linear Response
Maxwell’s equations are linear in the sources ($\rho,\mathbf{J}$). Consequently, the total field is the vector sum of contributions from each charge or current element—a principle called **superposition**. This linearity permits the use of Green’s functions; for a point charge the Green’s function of Poisson’s equation yields the Coulomb potential $V(\mathbf{r})=q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$.

### From Microscopic Charges to Macroscopic Currents
A steady current $I$ in a wire consists of drifting conduction electrons with number density $n$, charge $-e$, and drift velocity $\mathbf{v}_d$:  
$$I = n e A |\mathbf{v}_d|$$  
where $A$ is the cross‑sectional area. The magnetic field generated by this current follows from the Biot‑Savart law, which is itself a direct consequence of Ampere’s law applied to an infinitesimal current element $I d\mathbf{l}$:
$$d\mathbf{B}= \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^{2}}$$
Integrating over a straight, infinitely long wire gives the familiar result (see Worked Example 2).

### Energy and Momentum in Fields
The energy density stored in electromagnetic fields is  
$$u = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\varepsilon_0 E^{2}+ \tfrac{1}{\mu_0}B^{2}\bigr)$$  
and the **Poynting vector** $\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ describes the flux of electromagnetic energy. In a resistor, $\mathbf{S}$ points into the material, showing that electromagnetic energy from the field is converted into Joule heat—a direct link between the abstract field concepts and observable heating in conductors.

## Worked Examples
### Example 1: Electric Field of a Point Charge (Derivation)
**Problem:** A point charge $q=2\,\mu\text{C}$ sits at the origin. Find $\mathbf{E}$ at $r=10\,\text{cm}$.

**Solution:**  
1. Apply Gauss’s law to a spherical surface of radius $r$:  
   $$\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}=E(4\pi r^{2})=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$$  
   (symmetry gives $\mathbf{E}=E\hat{\mathbf{r}}$).  
2. Solve for $E$:  
   $$E=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}$$  
3. Insert constants: $\varepsilon_0=8.854\,187\,817\times10^{-12}\,\text{F}\,\text{m}^{-1}$, $q=2\times10^{-6}\,\text{C}$, $r=0.10\,\text{m}$:  
   $$E=\frac{2\times10^{-6}}{4\pi(8.854\times10^{-12})(0.10)^{2}}  
     =\frac{2\times10^{-6}}{1.112\times10^{-12}}  
     \approx1.80\times10^{6}\,\text{N}\,\text{C}^{-1}$$  
   (Note: the draft missed a factor of 10; correct value is $1.8\times10^{6}\,\text{N/C}$.)

### Example 2: Magnetic Field of an Infinite Straight Wire (Derivation)
**Problem:** A wire carries $I=5\,\text{A}$. Find $B$ at $r=5\,\text{cm}$.

**Solution:**  
1. Use Ampere’s law (magnetostatic, no displacement current):  
   $$\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l}=B(2\pi r)=\mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$$  
2. Solve:  
   $$B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$  
3. Insert $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\text{N}\,\text{A}^{-2}$, $I=5\,\text{A}$, $r=0.05\,\text{m}$:  
   $$B=\frac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times5}{2\pi\times0.05}  
     =\frac{2\times10^{-6}}{0.05}  
     =4.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$$  
   (The draft gave $2\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$; the correct value is $4.0\times10^{-5}\,\text{T}$.)

### Example 3: Lorentz Force on a Moving Charge
**Problem:** $q=2\,\mu\text{C}$, $\mathbf{v}=5\times10^{6}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,\text{m/s}$, $\mathbf{E}=1\times10^{5}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\text{N/C}$, $\mathbf{B}=1\times10^{-4}\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,\text{T}$. Compute $\mathbf{F}$.

**Solution:**  
$$\mathbf{F}=q\bigl(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\bigr)$$  
First compute $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$:  
$$\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}= (5\times10^{6}\hat{\mathbf{x}})\times(1\times10^{-4}\hat{\mathbf{z}})  
=5\times10^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\text{(since }\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}=-\hat{\mathbf{y}},\text{ but sign check: } \hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}}=-\hat{\mathbf{y}})$$  
Thus $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}= -5\times10^{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\text{N/C}$.  
Add to $\mathbf{E}$:  
$$\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}= (1\times10^{5}-5\times10^{2})\hat{\mathbf{y}}  
=9.95\times10^{4}\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\text{N/C}$$  
Finally:  
$$\mathbf{F}= (2\times10^{-6})(9.95\times10^{4})\hat{\mathbf{y}}  
\approx 0.199\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\,\text{N}$$

## Common Mistakes
| # | Misconception | Why It’s Wrong | Correct View |
|---|----------------|----------------|--------------|
| 1 | **“Electric field lines can cross.”** | Field lines represent the direction of $\mathbf{E}$; if they crossed, the field would have two directions at a point, violating the definition of a vector field. | Field lines never intersect; they diverge from positive charges and converge on negative charges. |
| 2 | **“Magnetic monopoles exist and act like electric charges.”** | No experimental evidence; Gauss’s law for magnetism $\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{B}=0$ forbids isolated magnetic charges. | Magnetic dipoles are the fundamental sources; monopoles would modify Maxwell’s equations. |
| 3 | **“The magnetic force does work on a charge.”** | The magnetic force $\mathbf{F}_m=q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ is always perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, so $\mathbf{F}_m\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. | Magnetic fields can change direction of motion but not kinetic energy; work is done by electric fields or induced emfs (Faraday’s law). |
| 4 | **“Using $B=\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ for a finite wire segment.”** | The formula assumes an infinitely long straight wire; edge effects become significant when length $\sim r$. | For finite segments, integrate the Biot‑Savart law or use the exact expression involving angles subtended by the segment. |
| 5 | **“Neglecting displacement current in high‑frequency circuits.”** | At GHz frequencies, $\varepsilon_0\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$ can dominate over conduction current, affecting wave propagation and impedance. | Include the $\varepsilon_0\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$ term in Ampere‑Maxwell law; this yields the correct wave speed $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. |

## Exercises
1. **(Easy)** A point charge $q=-4\,\mu\text{C}$ is located at $x=0$. Compute the electric field at $x=+15\,\text{cm}$.  
2. **(Medium)** A long straight wire carries $I=12\,\text{A}$. Find the magnetic field magnitude at $r=2\,\text{mm}$ and at $r=20\,\text{mm}$. Comment on the $1/r$ dependence.  
3. **(Hard)** A solenoid of length $L=0.30\,\text{m}$, radius $a=0.02\,\text{m}$, and $N=500$ turns carries a steady current $I=3\,\text{A}$.  
   a) Derive the magnetic field on the axis near the centre using Ampere’s law (treat as infinite).  
   b) Estimate the field at the end of the solenoid using the magnetic scalar potential of a finite solenoid (you may use the approximation $B_{\text{end}}\approx \frac{1}{2}B_{\text{centre}}$).  
   c) Compute the inductance $L_{\text{sol}}=\frac{N\Phi}{I}$ where $\Phi$ is the flux through one turn.

## Linux Connection
Electromagnetic phenomena are observable in Linux through hardware interfaces that expose voltages, currents, and magnetic fields to software. Below are concrete ways to interact with these subsystems.

### 1. Accessing I²C Magnetometers via the Industrial I/O (IIO) Subsystem
Many boards expose magnetometers (e.g., HMC5883L, QMC5883L) as IIO devices.

```bash
# List all IIO devices
iio_info -s

# Suppose the magnetometer appears as iio:device0
# Read raw X, Y, Z magnetometer values (units depend on device scale)
cat /sys/bus/iio/devices/iio:device0/in_magnet_x_raw
cat /bus/iio/devices/iio:device0/in_magnet_y_raw
cat /bus/iio/devices/iio:device0/in_magnet_z_raw

# Apply scaling factor (often found in *_scale files)
scale=$(cat /sys/bus/iio/devices/iio:device0/in_magnet_scale)
# Convert to tesla:
# Bx = raw_x * scale
```

### 2. Monitoring Power Consumption (Electromagnetic Energy Transfer)
The kernel’s **powercap** framework and tools like `powertop` report instantaneous power drawn from the supply—directly measuring the rate at which electromagnetic energy is converted to heat and mechanical work.

```bash
# Show power usage per device (requires root)
powertop --csv=powertop.csv

# Alternatively, read the RAPL interface (Intel)
cat /sys/class/powercap/intel-rapl:0/energy_uj
# Energy in microjoules; differentiate over time to get power.
```

### 3. Reading CPU Frequency Scaling (Voltage‑Frequency Relation)
CPU core frequency is adjusted based on the electromagnetic demands of the workload; the governor reflects the balance between electric power (voltage²·frequency) and thermal limits.

```bash
# Current frequency of each core
for cpu in /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu[0-9]*; do
    echo -n "$(basename $cpu): "
    cat $cpu/cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq
done

# Available governors
cat /sys/devices/system/cpu/cpu0/cpufreq/scaling_available_governors
```

### 4. Tracing Interrupts Caused by Electromagnetic Signals
Device interrupts (e.g., from network cards, timers) originate as voltage transitions on IRQ lines—electromagnetic events.

```bash
# View interrupt counts per CPU
cat /proc/interrupts

# Watch a specific IRQ (replace 42 with your device’s IRQ)
while true; do
    grep " 42:" /proc/interrupts
    sleep 1
done
```

### 5. Direct Memory‑Mapped I/O (MMIO) Access to Device Registers
Memory‑mapped registers control the electromagnetic state of peripherals (e.g., UART baud rate generators). Access them via `/dev/mem` or, preferably, through sysfs.

```bash
# Example: read the UART divisor latch (base address from lspci)
# Find UART base:
lspci -v | grep -i UART | head -1
# Assume base = 0x3f8 (legacy COM1)
# Map it safely using devmem2 (install via apt)
sudo devmem2 0x3f8 w   # read word
```

These examples show how the abstract quantities **E**, **B**, **V**, and **I** manifest as measurable files, device nodes, and kernel interfaces in a running Linux system.

## Why This Matters
Electromagnetism is the microscopic foundation of virtually every operation performed by a Linux system: the flow of electrons in conductors creates the currents that switch transistors, drive motors, and generate the voltage fluctuations that become interrupt signals. The kernel’s power management, I/O subsystems, and device drivers are all built to sense, control, or mitigate electromagnetic effects—whether by reading a magnetometer through IIO, limiting CPU frequency to stay within thermal‑electrical budgets, or accounting for the energy drawn from the supply via RAPL. Mastery of the underlying field equations lets engineers predict how changes in geometry, material, or frequency will alter these interfaces, enabling the design of more efficient hardware, tighter real‑time response, and lower‑power software. In short, a deep grasp of electromagnetism transforms the ability to *obtain* correct numbers from a *black box* into the capacity to *engineer* the system from first principles.
